## The receipt is lost before the warranty is

Thermal paper fades. Not eventually — within months, faster in heat, and faster
still in a wallet. A receipt printed at a market counter in April is frequently
unreadable by the time the device develops a fault in September.

This is not a small operational annoyance. It is the moment the shop's warranty
process breaks down. The customer arrives certain they bought the device there.
The shop has no way to confirm it without a receipt number, so the interaction
becomes a negotiation about trust rather than a lookup. Either the shop honours a
claim it cannot verify, or it refuses a customer who is probably telling the
truth. Both outcomes cost something.

## Send it where the customer already lives

In Nigeria, the answer is not email. It is WhatsApp.

WhatsApp is where customers already conduct business, coordinate with traders,
and keep records they care about. A receipt delivered there lands in a thread the
customer can search months later by shop name. It does not fade, it survives a
new phone, and it does not require the customer to have done anything deliberate
to preserve it.

The shop gets something equally valuable: a contact thread attached to a verified
purchase. That is the difference between a walk-in who is gone forever and a
customer the shop can reach when a relevant trade-in offer or a matching device
comes in. For a small retailer with no marketing budget, an opted-in list of
people who have actually bought something is the most valuable asset available,
and it accumulates as a by-product of issuing receipts properly.

It also matters that WhatsApp is already a sales channel rather than only a
delivery mechanism. Customers arrive through it, negotiate through it, and send
photos of devices through it. Issuing the receipt there closes the loop in the
same place the conversation started.

## Print anyway

None of this is an argument for going paperless.

Customers at a counter expect something in hand at the moment of payment, and a
shop that cannot produce one looks unserious. Counters already own thermal
printers. The correct posture is to support both, and to treat the channel as a
property of the document rather than a property of the system.

That means one document rendering correctly across four widths:

- **58mm** — the narrow thermal roll, common on compact counter printers
- **80mm** — the wider standard, more room for line items
- **A4** — formal invoices, credit agreements, anything that will be filed
- **WhatsApp** — the copy that survives

Designing for 58mm first is the useful discipline. If a receipt reads correctly on
the narrowest roll, everything wider is a relaxation of the same layout. Doing it
the other way round produces documents that were designed for A4 and get truncated
into nonsense on the roll.

## A receipt is not the only document

Once documents are treated as a first-class part of the system rather than a
print action, the list turns out to be longer than most POS software assumes:

- Quotation
- Invoice
- Waybill
- Credit agreement
- Repayment receipt
- Customer statement
- Supplier statement

Seven document types, each with a different audience and a different lifespan. A
waybill accompanies goods. A credit agreement has to survive the length of the
repayment. A customer statement is generated on demand, often during a dispute.

Treating all of them as renderings of structured data, rather than as separate
print templates, is what makes the WhatsApp channel almost free. The document
already exists as data; sending it to a thread instead of a printer is a delivery
decision, not a rewrite.

## What this generalises to

The principle is not about WhatsApp specifically. It is that the record of a
transaction should live where the customer will still be in six months, and that
the business should choose that place deliberately rather than defaulting to
whatever the printer emits.

In another market the answer might be email, or SMS, or a portal. In this one it
is WhatsApp, and building for it is not a workaround or a local quirk. It is
correctly reading where the customer actually is.
